Shenzhou (spacecraft)

Shenzhou (spacecraft) is an encyclopedia entry in the Wikimedia discovery collection.

Shenzhou is a Chinese spacecraft developed for the China Manned Space Program. Debuting with the uncrewed Shenzhou 1 on 19 November 1999, the spacecraft has logged 23 total flights. Human spaceflight operations began on 15 October 2003 with Shenzhou 5, spanning 17 crewed missions, the most recent of which is Shenzhou 23.

Background

Shenzhou ( Chinese : 神舟 ; pinyin : Shénzhōu , / ˈ ʃ ɛ n ˈ dʒ oʊ / shen- JOH ; see § Etymology ) is a Chinese spacecraft developed for the China Manned Space Program . Debuting with the uncrewed Shenzhou 1 on 19 November 1999, the spacecraft has logged 23 total flights. Human spaceflight operations began on 15 October 2003 with Shenzhou 5 , spanning 17 crewed missions, the most recent of which is Shenzhou 23 . Its design is based on the Soyuz , but Shenzhou is larger and incorporates modernized systems. Like Soyuz, Shenzhou is a single-use vehicle composed of three modules; a reentry module housing the crew during launch and reentry , an orbital module which provides additional living space and storage, and a service module for propulsion and power; the latter two are discarded before reentry. All Shenzhou missions have launched on Long March 2F variants from Jiuquan Satellite Launch Center and landed in Inner Mongolia . Up to two Shenzhou craft are typically docked to China's Tiangong modular space station , complemented by an unmanned Tianzhou cargo spacecraft. Shenzhou missions previously docked with the Tiangong-1 and Tiangong-2 space laboratories. It is slated to be replaced by the next-generation Mengzhou , currently in development, which has a two-module configuration.

Etymology

The name Shenzhou ( 神舟 ; Shénzhōu ) means "divine vessel" and evokes a vessel traveling on the Heavenly River ( 天河 ), the Classical Chinese name for the Milky Way . It is also a homophonous pun on the identically pronounced 神州 , a poetic name for China meaning "divine realm". The calligraphy of the spacecraft's name and its Long March 2F carrier rocket ( 神 箭 ; Shénjiàn ; "divine arrow") was written by President Jiang Zemin . Shenzhou was selected in 1994 as the name for the first Chinese manned spacecraft after a call for suggested names among participating organizations in the space program. Other proposed names included 华夏 ; Huáxià ; "Huaxia", 九州 ; Jiǔzhōu ; "Nine Provinces" (both archaic and poetic names for China) as well as 腾 龙 ; Ténglóng ; "Soaring Dragon".

Origins and initial planning

China's first efforts at human spaceflight began in 1967 with the Shuguang spacecraft, then projected to launch in 1973. Shuguang was a two-person design that broadly resembled the Gemini spacecraft . Despite the successful launch in 1970 of Dong Fang Hong 1 , China's first satellite, funding shortages resulted in the cancellation of the Shuguang spacecraft in 1972 and the rest of the program in 1980. After the establishment of the 863 Program in 1986, intensive meetings by a committee convened by the program to evaluate human spaceflight options began in 1988 and continued until 1991. According to China Youth Daily , the committee considered the possibility of building a spaceplane -like system for the nascent manned space program, but ultimately rejected the concept on the grounds of cost and technical feasibility. Discussants pointed out that the American Space Shuttle incurred launch costs far beyond China's financial capabilities, the Soviet Buran had only flown once, and ESA was struggling with the development of their Hermes spaceplane. With the approval of Premier Li Peng , it was decided that the program would utilize a simpler space capsule design with a target launch around 1998-99 ( 争 八 保 九 ; zhēng bā bǎo jiǔ ; "strive for 1998 and guarantee 1999"). Development

China's crewed space program was officially relaunched on 21 September 1992 with the approval of Project 921 by the Politburo Standing Committee . "Phase One" of the project focused on developing the manned spacecraft that would eventually become Shenzhou. Primary development of the spacecraft was led by the China Academy of Space Technology (CAST), with the Shanghai Academy of Spaceflight Technology (SAST) responsible for its service module and three subsystems. Subsequently, China and Russia concluded a series of bilateral agreements on space cooperation (including human spaceflight) in 1992, 1994, and 1996. Russian space officials state that China purchased a bare-bones Soyuz reentry capsule from Energia and various pieces of hardware (some obsolete) in 1995, but there was little exchange between space-related corporations after the first half of 1990s. According to Brian Harvey, the Chinese program acquired a spacecraft life-support system, a Sokol space suit , a docking module, a Kurs docking system from Russia in addition to the stripped-down Soyuz reentry capsule. Another Chinese report additionally notes the purchase of an obsolete Soyuz launch-escape motor for $800,000 that turned out to be empty.
