COMX-35

COMX-35 is an encyclopedia entry in the Platforms collection.

Background

The COMX-35 is a home computer released in 1983 and manufactured in Hong Kong by COMX World Operations Ltd . It was one of the very few systems built around the RCA 1802 microprocessor , the same processor used in some space probes . Sold as an inexpensive family machine, it had a keyboard with an integrated joystick in place of cursor keys, 35 KB of memory and a BASIC interpreter in ROM . It offered no graphics mode, although its character set could be redefined to simulate one. The COMX-35 was released in the Netherlands , the United Kingdom , Sweden , New Zealand , Australia , Finland , Norway , Italy , Singapore , Turkey and the People's Republic of China . In the United Kingdom, the computer was on sale by August 1983 at a price of £120, where its construction was received favourably but its BASIC was found slow and unusual in its dialect. The machine became best known in the Netherlands, where the importer West Electronics translated its documentation into Dutch and distributed most of its software free of charge. According to the Home Computer Museum, the Dutch importer later went bankrupt following an unfavourable test by the consumer organisation Consumentenbond . In Sweden, the computer was sold by Noxon AB as the noXon comx 35. A second model, the COMX PC1, improved the keyboard and added a joystick port.

History

COMX World Operations marketed the machine as a family home computer at a time when most low-cost systems were built around the 6502 or Z80 processors, which made the choice of the RCA 1802 unusual for a home computer. In the United Kingdom the computer was on sale by August 1983 at a price of £120. Your Computer examined the machine in its issue of August 1983. The reviewer, Kathleen Peel, judged it solidly built and well equipped, noting that it arrived with cassette leads, a television cable, a power supply, and a manual for its BASIC, and that it ran unusually cool. Her main objection was the interpreter's speed, which took roughly four times as long as the ZX Spectrum and the Oric to run through the magazine's timing tests. She also observed that the BASIC dialect departed from common practice, that the manual described no printer commands, and that the expansion connector was undocumented. A printer interface, a disc drive, a speech synthesiser and a memory expansion were all announced as forthcoming at that point. In the Netherlands, the computer was imported by West Electronics. Reviewing it for Radio Bulletin in September 1984, H. J. C. Otten placed the COMX-35 among a growing number of home computers priced below a thousand guilders and aimed at hobbyists and education rather than at serious applications. Memory

The COMX-35 has 35 KB of RAM. It consists of 32 KB of User RAM of which roughly 30 KB is available for BASIC code, with the rest reserved for use by the BASIC system ROM. The remaining 3 KB of RAM is used by the Video Interface System.

Video interface system

The COMX-35 uses the RCA CDP1869 and CDP1870 Video Interface System (VIS), consisting of the CDP1869 address and sound generator and the CDP1870 colour video generator. The COMX-35 automatically detects the refresh rate . The VIS runs on 5.626 MHz in PAL and 5.67 MHz in NTSC . This frequency is halved and used to clock the CPU. The VIS is also responsible for the timing of the interrupts and non display period. Video memory can only be accessed during the non display period, which allows for execution of 2160 machine cycles in PAL and 1574 cycles in NTSC . To maximize access to the video memory, the program can be paused until the non display period by checking EF1 in a loop. The video memory is divided into 1 KB RAM character memory and 2 KB RAM page memory. The page memory stores the ASCII code for each character position on the screen. The screen had 960 characters where position 0 (left top corner) could be accessed by memory location $F800 (before scrolling). The character memory stored the character definition of each ASCII character and can be accessed at memory locations $F400-$F7FF .
