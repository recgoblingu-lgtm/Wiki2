Spontaneous emission

Spontaneous emission is an encyclopedia entry in the Wikimedia discovery collection.

Spontaneous emission is the process in which a quantum mechanical system transitions from an excited energy state to a lower energy state and emits a quantized amount of energy in the form of a photon into a field that was previously devoid of radiation. Lasers start via spontaneous emission, then during continuous operation work by stimulated emission.

Background

Spontaneous emission is the process in which a quantum mechanical system (such as a molecule , an atom or a subatomic particle ) transitions from an excited energy state to a lower energy state (e.g., its ground state ) and emits a quantized amount of energy in the form of a photon into a field that was previously devoid of radiation. Lasers start via spontaneous emission, then during continuous operation work by stimulated emission . Albert Einstein discussed spontaneous emission in a series of papers starting in 1916, which first predicted the phenomenon of stimulated emission and introduced the Einstein Coefficients . Einstein's quantum theory of radiation anticipated ideas later expressed in quantum electrodynamics and quantum optics by several decades. Later, after the formal discovery of quantum mechanics in 1926, the rate of spontaneous emission was accurately described from first principles by Paul Dirac in his quantum theory of radiation, the precursor to the theory which he later called quantum electrodynamics . Contemporary physicists, when asked to give a physical explanation for spontaneous emission, generally invoke the zero-point energy of the electromagnetic field. Theory

Spontaneous transitions cannot be modeled in the absence of an electromagnetic field, even with quantum-mechanical treatment of the atom. The stationary quantum states of an atom are orthogonal: the overlap of the wavefunctions between an excited state and the ground state of the atom is zero. Thus, in the absence of a quantized electromagnetic field operator, the excited state atom cannot decay to the ground state. In order to explain spontaneous transitions, the state of the electromagnetic field must be included in the system. In quantum electrodynamics (or QED), the electromagnetic field has a ground state , the QED vacuum , which can mix with the excited stationary states of the atom. As a result of this interaction, the stationary state of the atom is no longer a eigenstate of the combined system of the atom plus electromagnetic field. The transition from the electronic excited state to the electronic ground state mixes with the transition of the electromagnetic field from the ground state to an excited state, a field state with one photon in it. Spontaneous emission in free space depends upon vacuum fluctuations to get started. Although there is only one electronic transition from the excited state to ground state, there are many ways in which the electromagnetic field may go from the ground state to a one-photon state.
