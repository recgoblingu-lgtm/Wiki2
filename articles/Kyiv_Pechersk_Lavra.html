Kyiv Pechersk Lavra

Kyiv Pechersk Lavra is an encyclopedia entry in the Wikimedia discovery collection.

The Kyiv Pechersk Lavra, also known as the Kyiv Monastery of the Caves, is a historic lavra or large monastery of Eastern Christianity that gave its name to the Pecherskyi District where it is located in Kyiv.

Background

The Kyiv Pechersk Lavra ( Ukrainian : Києво-Печерська лавра , romanized : Kyievo-Pecherska Lavra ), also known as the Kyiv Monastery of the Caves , is a historic lavra or large monastery of Eastern Christianity that gave its name to the Pecherskyi District where it is located in Kyiv . Since its foundation as a cave monastery during the 11th century, the Lavra has been a preeminent center of Eastern Christianity in Eastern Europe. Its printing house was an early part of the development of printing in Ukraine .

Etymology and other names

Ukrainian : печера , romanized : pechera means lit. cave , which in turn derived from Proto-Slavic *реktera with the same meaning. Ukrainian: лавра , romanized: lavra is used to describe high-ranking male monasteries for monks of the Eastern Orthodox Church . Therefore, the name of the monastery is also translated as Kyiv Cave Monastery, Kyiv Caves Monastery or the Kyiv Monastery of the Caves (from Ukrainian: на печерах ).

Foundation and early history

The Primary Chronicle contains contradictory information as to when the monastery was founded: in 1051, or in 1074. Anthony , a monk on Mount Athos , originally from Liubech of the Principality of Chernigov , returned to Rus' and settled in Kyiv as a missionary of monastic tradition to Kyivan Rus'. He chose a cave at the Berestov Mount that overlooked the Dnipro river , where metropolitan Hilarion had reportedly resided during his youth, and a community of disciples soon grew. Prince Iziaslav I of Kyiv (1024–1078) ceded the whole mount to the Anthonite monks who founded a monastery built by architects from Constantinople . Supported by numerous princes and boyars , the monastery served as the most important centre of religion and culture in Rus'. Its monks produced important works of literature, including the Primary Chronicle and Kyiv Caves Patericon . It also housed renowned builders and artists, and the monastery life inspired numerous tales and legends. At the same time, it suffered from numerous attacks: in 1096 the monastery was plundered by the Cumans , and in 1169 sacked by prince Andrey Bogolyubsky . In 1203 it was once again plundered by forces of prince Rurik Rostislavich and his allies. In 1240 the monastery fell victim to Mongol invaders commanded by Batu Khan , and in 1416 was burned down by forces of Golden Horde ruler Edigey . After being rebuilt by prince Simeo

Baroque era and Russian rule

Following reconstruction, during the second half of 16th century the monastery received rights of stauropegion from the Patriarch of Constantinople , and started bearing the name of Lavra. During that period, six separate monasteries were united under its leadership. After the Union of Brest , the monastery became an object of dispute between the Orthodox and Catholic communities. In 1613, the Orthodox established their control after receiving support of the princely Ostrogski family . In the 17th century, under the leadership of archimandrites Eliseus Pletenetsky , Zacharias Kopystensky and Peter Mohyla , the monastery stood at the heart of Ukrainian national identity. Kyiv Caves Patericon , which was created by Lavra's monks and soon became a popular reading around the whole Eastern Europe , contributed to the emergence of the symbolic image of Kyiv as a capital of Eastern Orthodoxy . Lavra's printing house, established by Pletenetskyi in the 1620s, started the process of Kyiv's cultural revival, and the monastery's school, founded by Mohyla, introduced European educational trends of the time, leading to a radical reform of education. During the Baroque era Kyiv Pechersk Lavra flourished as a centre of arts and spirituality, and pilgrimage to Kyiv was seen by some as more preferable than visiting Jerusalem .
