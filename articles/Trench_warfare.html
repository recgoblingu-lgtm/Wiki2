Trench warfare

Trench warfare is an encyclopedia entry in the Wikimedia discovery collection.

Trench warfare is a type of land warfare using occupied lines largely comprising military trenches, in which combatants are well-protected from the enemy's fire and are substantially sheltered from artillery. Although used in antiquity it fully developed in the 17th century. During the American Civil War (1861–1865), particularly during the sieges of Vicksburg and Petersburg, modern firepower clashed with outdated infantry tactics. Soldiers began digging in to avoid the deadly shelling. This already signaled the end of classic open-field battles. This development ultimately reached its most extreme and deadly form in World War I (1914–1918). Through the combined use of machine guns, rapid-fire artillery, and barbed wire, the front lines—particularly on the Western Front—became frozen in a kilometer-long, deeply echeloned system of trenches. The trench was now no longer merely a means of siege but, as a defensive infrastructure, determined the entire course of the industrialized war of attrition.

Background

Although used in antiquity it fully developed in the 17th century. This development ultimately reached its most extreme and deadly form in World War I (1914–1918). Through the combined use of machine guns, rapid-fire artillery, and barbed wire, the front lines—particularly on the Western Front—became frozen in a kilometer-long, deeply echeloned system of trenches. The trench was now no longer merely a means of siege but, as a defensive infrastructure, determined the entire course of the industrialized war of attrition.

Origin

An early mention of the trench dates back to the 5th century B.C. by the Greek historian Thucydides in his work The History of the Peloponnesian War. Here he states: A trench was dug all round the temple and the consecrated ground, and the earth thrown up from the excavation was made to do duty as a wall. […] Meanwhile the Syracusans marched out and began a second stockade, starting from the city, across the middle of the marsh, digging a trench alongside to make it impossible for the Athenians to carry their wall down to the sea. Further mentions come from Aristotle In Nicomachean Ethics he mentioned that "generals" draw their soldiers in front of the trenches. A more general use of trenches can be found in the accounts of Roman historians. Among them are Polybius who refers to trenches several times in his work about the punic wars and Flavius Josephus mentioning the use of trenches during the First Jewish-Roman War. The most comprehensive report comes from Caesar. In De Bello Gallico he describes in detail the constrution of trenches during the Siege of Alesia. In order to protect the soldiers working on the main siege constructions a 20 feet wide trench was dugged. Additionally two further inner trenches each 15 feet broad and 15 feet deep were dugged and flooded. American Civil War

During the U.S. Civil, particularly during the Overland and Petersburg campaigns, an early form of trench warfare, which would come to define World War I, developed. Although the trenches often appeared to merge into one another to on-site observers, the Union fortifications were essentially divided into three levels: the rear area, the main line of battle, and the front area with firing holes on both sides. The trenches were dug wide enough for soldiers to sit or stand in them. Toward the enemy, slightly raised logs were placed to protect the soldiers' heads and shoulders while they fired. At regular intervals, there were forts and batteries made of wood and earth to house artillery and siege mortars. Many positions, especially forts and batteries, had so-called bomb-proofs. These were deep holes in the ground, reinforced with tree trunks and covered with wood and earth, designed to provide protection against artillery bombardments. As there were no barb wire or other obstacles that would effectivly have stopped an attacker the main advantage for the defender were the open glacis that had to be crossed. This would give the defender enough time to fire as many shots as possible at close range from relative cover and demoralize the attacker before he reaches the objective.

First World War

Although technology had dramatically changed the nature of warfare by 1914, the armies of the major combatants had not fully absorbed the implications. Fundamentally, as the range and rate of fire of rifled small-arms increased, a defender shielded from enemy fire (in a trench, at a house window, behind a large rock, or behind other cover) was often able to kill several approaching foes before they closed around the defender's position. Attacks across open ground became even more dangerous after the introduction of rapid-firing artillery , exemplified by the "French 75" , machine gun and high explosive fragmentation rounds. The increases in firepower had outstripped the ability of infantry (or even cavalry ) to cover the ground between firing lines and the ability of armour to withstand fire. It would take a revolution in mobility to change that. The French and the Imperial German Army adopted different tactical doctrines : the French relied on the attack with speed and surprise ( Attaque à outrance ) and the Germans relied on firepower , investing heavily in howitzers and machine guns . The British lacked an official tactical doctrine, with an officer corps that rejected theory in favour of pragmatism.
