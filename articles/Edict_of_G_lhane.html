Background

The Gülhane Hatt-ı Şerifi ("Supreme Edict of Gülhane") or Tanzimât Fermânı ("Imperial Edict of Reorganization") was a proclamation by Ottoman Sultan Abdülmecid I in 1839 that launched the Tanzimât period of reforms and reorganization in the Ottoman Empire . It is named after Gülhane Park , where the edict was first proclaimed. The 125th anniversary of the edict was depicted on a former Turkish postcard stamp. The proclamation was issued at the behest of reformist Grand Vizier Mustafa Reşid Pasha . It promised reforms such as the abolition of tax farming , reform of conscription , and guarantee of rights to all Ottoman citizens regardless of religion or ethnic group. The goal of the decree was to help modernize the empire militarily and socially so that it could compete with the Great Powers of Europe . It also was hoped the reforms would win over the disaffected parts of the empire, especially in the Ottoman controlled parts of Europe, which were largely Christian . At the time of the edict, millets (independent communal law-courts) had gained a large amount of religious autonomy within the empire, threatening the central government. This edict, along with the subsequent Imperial Reform Edict of 1856, was therefore an early step towards the empire's goal of Ottomanism , or a unified national and legal Ottoman identity. It was published in the Takvim-i Vekayi in Ottoman Turkish.

Issues facing Mahmud II's Regime

In the years prior to the production of the Gulhane Edict, Sultan Mahmud II faced several threats to his sovereignty that demonstrated the need for modernizing reform. A series of military losses, the inefficiency of the decentralized Ottoman government, and the failures of the Iltizam to effectively collect taxes and address disparities between Muslim and Non-Muslim populations prompted Mahmud II to begin work on the Edict. The Ottoman Empire faced rapid social and economic decline in the decades prior to 1839, and Mahmud II believed European-based reform efforts were the solution to these issues. Although the Edict didn't materialize any of the reforms mentioned, it served as a framework for Mahmud II's son, Abdülmecid I , to enact his modernizing reforms during the Tanzimat period. A series of Janissary revolts during 1807 deposed Selim III , Mahmud II's brother, from the Ottoman throne, and the threat of an independent military body on Mahmud II's political power would eventually prompt him to abolish the Janissaries in 1826. The military inefficiencies demonstrated during the Greek War of Independence led the Ottoman Empire to lose vast amounts of Western territory in the years before the Edict.
