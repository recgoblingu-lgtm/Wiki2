Background

The Reality Construction Kit , also called Reality Software Construction Kit or Reality Game Engine , is a game creation system for the Amiga computer platform and published in 1996.

Design

Reality is a piece of software allowing the creation of video games for the Commodore Amiga platform. The user configures the parameters of their game using a graphical user interface, which includes tools for working with graphics, sound, and variables. The resulting games target the standard OCS Amiga configuration. Different genres of games can be made using different Skeleton Disks, each of which serve as a framework. Included with the original distribution were Skeleton Disks for two genres: a side-scrolling shoot 'em up game, and an adventure game in the style of Dizzy series . Further Skeleton Disks were planned for release which would have added additional genres.

Background

In the 1990s, GamesMaster, the first UK television programme dedicated to video games, ran a contest sponsored by publisher Europress , challenging viewers to create their own video game using the company's product, AMOS BASIC . The winner was one Brian Bell of Northern Ireland, whose game Charlie Chimp was distributed as a cover disk with the May 1993 issue of Amiga Format magazine in the form of a three-level demo. Bell reportedly sold around 4,000 copies of the full 30-level version at £5 apiece, earning more than £20,000, a moderate success at the time for an independently produced game. Bell was able to pivot his fame into the Mr. AMOS Club, a usergroup and disk magazine . The disk magazine ran for six issues from May 1993 to early 1994. He marketed the club as a way to introduce people into the world of independent game development using AMOS BASIC, which was considered the most beginner-friendly Amiga programming language for that purpose. He also used the magazine to market various other games of his produced in AMOS, including Wrath of Gwendor and Seemore Doolittle's Underwater Capers , as well as various disks of game development assets. The Mr. AMOS Club disk magazine was praised for its included code, particularly for the quality of game engines its provided, which were well-written and performant despite AMOS' reputation for ordinarily poor performance.

Development and release

Development on Reality began in 1994, and included Brian Bell and at least one other coder. Reality was written in AMOS BASIC , a language known for its beginner-friendliness and focus on game development. It followed in the spirit of such tools as the Shoot-'Em-Up Construction Kit (1989), which allowed users to easily create their own video game to a single genre without writing any code. Reality was previewed in in the August 1996 issue of CU Amiga magazine, which depicted a point-and-click adventure. Full-page advertisements for Reality first appeared in the August 1996 issue of CU Amiga and November 1996 issue of Amiga Format . These promised that Reality would allow code-free development of commercial-quality games in nearly any genre. The product sold for £29.99 (equivalent to $60 in 2025), including the engines for two genres of games. It was distributed as six-disk set including a Master Disk, three Skeleton Disks (Shoot-Em-Up, Adventure, and an Education disk which doubles as documentation and marketing), a Music disk, and a Sound disk, plus a short photocopied documentation booklet. The two game Skeleton Disks also featured the sample games Seemore Doolittle's Underwater Caper's and Charlie Chimp and the Treasure of Tutankhamun .
