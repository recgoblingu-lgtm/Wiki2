Background

The benefactive case ( abbreviated ben , or sometimes b when it is a core argument ) is a grammatical case typically used where English would use "for", "for the benefit of", or "intended for." For instance, "She opened the door for Tom " or "This book is for Bob " would both be examples of benefactive case (although generally unmarked in English). The benefactive case expresses that the referent of the noun it marks receives the benefit of the situation expressed by the clause. This meaning is often incorporated through dative case . In Latin , this type of dative is called the dativus commodi .

Basque

Basque has a benefactive case ending in - entzat, from the genitive - en and essive - tzat .

Quechua

Quechua is another example of a language with the benefactive case. Some researchers have argued that the benefactive and genitive cases , both marked with the ending -paq, have become indistinguishable. The following example is from Cuzco Quechua: Pi-paq-taq Who- BEN - EMP chay that punchu-ta-ri poncho- ACC - CNTR awa-sha-nki weave- PR - 2SG Pi-paq-taq chay punchu-ta-ri awa-sha-nki Who-BEN-EMP that poncho-ACC-CNTR weave-PR-2SG “For who are you weaving that poncho?”

Tangkhul-Naga

Tangkhul-Naga (from the Tibeto-Burman group of languages) has the benefactive case marker -wiʋaŋ . The case marker comes from the combining the genitive case marker - wùi and the suffix - vaη .
