Background

Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael , 526 U.S. 137 (1999), is a United States Supreme Court case which held that the Daubert standard applies to expert testimony from non-scientists. The opinion also clarified that the standard's application in part depends on the facts of the case and of the particular nature of the expert. The Daubert standard was first articulated by the Court in Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc. (1993) and describes how federal courts should determine whether to admit expert testimony. The Daubert standard directs lower courts to examine a witness's methodology and qualifications for relevance and reliability based on a non-exhaustive list of factors. However, the Court's initial description of the Daubert standard focused on testimony from scientists and involving scientific methodologies, and until Kumho Tire , it remained unclear whether and how the standard applied to non-scientist experts like engineers. Kumho Tire resolved this by holding that, while the larger requirements that expert testimony be relevant and reliable were universal, a court in evaluating an expert may consider any number of factors, including those not listed in the original Daubert opinion, depending on the case. Legal background

In the American legal system, parties to litigation may sometimes prepare expert witnesses to testify concerning technical or scientific matters. At the time of Kumho Tire , Federal Rule of Evidence 702 described the standard for admitting expert witnesses to testify in federal court as so: If scientific, technical, or other specialized knowledge will assist the trier of fact to understand the evidence or to determine a fact in issue, a witness qualified as an expert by knowledge, skill, experience, training, or education, may testify thereto in the form of an opinion or otherwise. For many decades, including before the Federal Rules of Evidence were passed, courts admitted expert witnesses based on whether their methods were generally accepted in their discipline, in what was known as the Frye standard . Following an increase in toxic tort and medical litigation in the 1980s and a concomitant increase in expert testimony, some of which was considered dubious, legal experts began to call for more stringent requirements for the admission of experts. Some courts began to adopt stricter standards in response, though other courts maintained a relatively low level of scrutiny. In 1993, the Supreme Court decided Daubert v. Facts and initial procedural history

On July 6, 1993, a minivan transporting eight members of the Carmichael family, including the driver, experienced a rear tire blowout and overturned on a road in Alabama. One passenger died, while everyone else inside the vehicle was injured. The Carmichaels subsequently sued Samyang Tires , the manufacturer and distributor of the blown-out tire, alleging that it was defective. Crucial to the Carmichaels' strategy was a tire failure analyst named Dennis Carlson, who provided deposition testimony opining that, based on certain observations he had made of the tire's remains, the cause of the blowout was likely a manufacturing defect rather than improper inflation or misuse. Carlson's method for determining whether blowouts were caused by manufacturing defect or user error rested on visually examining tire remains for the presence of four signs of defective manufacture; Carlson claimed that the tire was defective because it exhibited two of the four signs. Carlson's knowledge on the subject came from his past experience designing tires for Michelin , as well as a master's degree in mechanical engineering from Georgia Tech . Samyang moved to exclude Carlson's testimony, arguing that his methodology for assessing the causes of tire failure failed the Daubert standard. Briefs and oral arguments

Due to concerns about the case's potential impact on the admissibility of evidence in both criminal and civil trials, several organizations filed " friend of the court " briefs, with nineteen such briefs in total. A group of law enforcement organizations filed a brief arguing that the methods used to gather forensic evidence in crime scene investigations should be exempt from the Daubert standard. Briefs favoring Kumho Tire were submitted by the Justice Department and several prominent legal organizations, such as the Atlantic Legal Foundation , Washington Legal Foundation , and United States Chamber of Commerce , as well as groups associated with the tort , automotive, and engineering sectors, such as the American Tort Reform Association , Rubber Manufacturers Association , and National Association of Manufacturers ; while briefs supporting the Carmichaels were submitted by the Association of Trial Lawyers of America , Center for Auto Safety , and Public Citizen , among other advocacy groups. The Court held oral arguments on December 7, 1998. In an article reflecting on the decision, the lawyer representing Kumho Tire at the oral arguments characterized the justices as largely sympathetic to his case, excepting Stephen Breyer , who "appeared equally testy for both sides;" John Paul Stevens , who "seemed uncommitted;" and Clarence Thomas , who did not ask any questions at all.
