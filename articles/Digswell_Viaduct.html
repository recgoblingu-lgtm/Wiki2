Background

The Digswell Viaduct , also known as the Welwyn Viaduct and officially the Welwyn Railway Viaduct , is a railway viaduct that carries two tracks of the East Coast Main Line over the Mimram Valley in Hertfordshire in the East of England. A prominent local landmark, it is located between Welwyn Garden City and Welwyn North railway stations, and is located above the village of Digswell and the River Mimram . Designed by father and son William and Joseph Cubitt , and engineered by Thomas Brassey , the viaduct opened in 1850 and has been a Grade II* listed structure since 1980. The viaduct has forty arches and was originally built from red facing bricks, with blue facing bricks added in the 1930s. Metal gantries were appended to the side of the viaduct in 1980 as part of the electrification of the Great Northern route . At approximately 1,560 feet (475 m) in length, and around 100 ft (30 m) in height, it was one of the largest works on the Great Northern Railway when it opened.

Design

The viaduct carries two tracks of the East Coast Main Line across the River Mimram and its valley. The viaduct was styled to be similar to Roman aqueducts , and was one of the largest works on the GNR when it was built. It has forty arches, and is built from thirteen million red bricks, which were fired using clay that had been mined during the construction of the viaduct. The records of Earl Cowper , who owned the land, gave the precise number of bricks produced on the site as 13,975,638, but some of these would have been used to build Welwyn North station and line the Welwyn Tunnels . Each pier was built with 65 piles driven into the ground by means of a steam-fuelled piling engine. The viaduct is the first perfectly level section of the ECML out of King's Cross. The waterproofing originally consisted of a bitumen membrane , which the brickwork was covered with so that the water would drain into cast iron pipes at the centre of each pier. The drainage system had become clogged over time but this could not be fixed as the pipes were inaccessibly positioned inside the viaduct. Furthermore, the membrane's efficacy had deteriorated to a point that water was leaking through and running down inside the brickwork. These systems were replaced in 1986. Dimensions

The height and length of the viaduct are not recorded consistently. The National Heritage List for England (NHLE) entry for the viaduct, citing The Railway Heritage of Britain , gives its length as 519 yards (475 m), which is the same as the figure in Welwyn & Welwyn Garden City in 50 Buildings. The original plans suggested the viaduct would be 521 yards (476 m), and British Railways' concurred with this length when asked by the authors of Welwyn's Railways , who, having seen various figures, settled on "about 520 yards (480 m)", which is the figure in Eastern and Central England volume of Civil Engineering Heritage, in Wrottesley's The Great Northern Railway , and in an official document published by East Herts District Council . Charles H. Grinling's The History of the Great Northern Railway, 1845–1922 suggests the completely different length of 497 yards (454 m). This figure and 520 yards are suggested in Rook's River Mimram. There is no disagreement between sources in the length of a span at 30 ft (9 m). The height of the viaduct increased as a result of electrification. The pre-electrification figure given in Wrottesley's The Great Northern Railway is a maximum of 89 feet (27 m) above the ground. 1848–1850: Construction and opening

The viaduct was built for the Great Northern Railway (GNR) between 1848 and 1850, in order to carry their line between King's Cross and Welwyn (now Welwyn North), although they planned for this to extend to York . The contract had begun in 1846, and bricks were made from 1847. It is the largest viaduct on the GNR's main line. The viaduct was designed by railway engineers William Cubitt , the GNR's chief engineer, and his son Joseph Cubitt . Some sources attribute it to Lewis Cubitt , who was of no close relation to William and Joseph. The railway engineer George Hudson opposed the viaduct's construction, saying that it would collapse into the valley due to the nature of the ground, but also because he wanted to protect his monopoly on the Midlands ' railways. In order to support the viaduct, a 2,200,000-cubic-yard (1,700,000 m) trench was excavated and then packed with clay and mortar. Due to the inconsistent ground in the valley, eleven of the piers also had to have timber added to their structure. The construction of the viaduct came at a cost of £69,397, which was equivalent to £8,077,000 in 2025. This was a cost per mile of 133.20, ten times more expensive than the rest of the line.
